Why the Rankings Changed

Why Google Changed Search Forever (1998)

In the late 1990s, the World Wide Web was experiencing explosive growth, but finding reliable information was becoming increasingly impossible. Early search engines like AltaVista, Lycos, Excite, and WebCrawler indexed web pages by analyzing the keywords within their text and metadata.

Before Google: Keyword Stuffing Chaos

Pre-1998 search engines operated on a naive assumption: the more times a webpage repeats a word, the more relevant it must be.

Spammers quickly exploited this loophole. Webmasters created low-quality landing pages repeating the word pizza hundreds of times in tiny fonts or white text on white backgrounds. In our dataset, pizza-pizza-pizza.com uses this exact technique to artificially grab the #1 spot in traditional search engines.

Google's 1998 PageRank Breakthrough

Stanford Ph.D. students Larry Page and Sergey Brin realized that the web was not just an unstructured document repository—it was a directed social network of trust.

Drawing an analogy from academic citations (where influential research papers are cited by other distinguished scholars), PageRank treated every hyperlink as a vote of confidence.

“In academic citation analysis, you don't just count citations; you weight each citation by the importance of the paper that cited it. PageRank translates this exact citation graph to the World Wide Web.”
— Larry Page & Sergey Brin, Stanford University Computer Science Department (1998)

The Two Pillars of PageRank

The PageRank formula revolutionized information retrieval through two core principles:

  1. Endorsement by Citation: A webpage gains authority not from what it claims about itself, but from how many other independent websites point to it. In our dataset, best-pizza-pune.com has 4 incoming links from food guides, student blogs, and recipe websites.
  2. Link Dilution Protection (C(Ti)): If an authority page links to 100 websites, its vote is split 100 ways. If it links to only 2 websites, each gets half of its endorsement. This prevents link farms from multiplying influence out of thin air.

The Random Surfer Model & Damping Factor (d = 0.85)

Imagine an authentic 1998 internet user browsing the World Wide Web:

  • 85% of the time (d = 0.85): The surfer clicks a hyperlink on their current page.
  • 15% of the time (1 - d = 0.15): The surfer gets bored and teleports to a completely random webpage by typing a new URL in their browser address bar.

Mathematically, PageRank calculates the stationary probability distribution of where this random surfer will be located after browsing indefinitely. Highly cited, trusted hubs naturally accumulate the vast majority of visits.

Interactive Analogy: PageRank as Water Flow in Pipes

Think of each webpage as a water tank and each hyperlink as a pipe. At every iteration, tanks pump water out through their outgoing pipes into the tanks they link to.

Because pizza-pizza-pizza.com has zero incoming pipes, its tank never receives water from anyone else. It only maintains a baseline puddle (1 - d = 0.15). Meanwhile, best-pizza-pune.com has four separate pipes pumping water into it, making it the deepest, most authoritative reservoir on the web.

PageRank Algorithm Lab & Convergence Math

Inspect the rigorous mathematical mechanics of PageRank: iterative matrix operations, convergence logs, and dataset topology.

1. The Canonical Formula

PR(A) = (1 - d) + d × ∑ [ PR(Ti) / C(Ti) ]

Where PR(A) is the score of page A; d = 0.85 is the damping factor; Ti are pages linking into A; and C(Ti) is the total number of outgoing links from page Ti.

2. Iterative Convergence Table (Damping Factor d = 0.85)

PageRank starts with all pages initialized to 1.0 (average score) and updates values iteratively until the maximum delta (Δ) drops below ε = 10-6.
